# Commission guidance on the AI Act - sources and extracted corpus

Collected 2026-11-03 from official europa.eu domains only. Non-binding interpretive material
(`meta.binding: false`). Rebuild JSON from the downloaded files with:

    backend/.venv/bin/python -I scripts/build_guidance.py [slug ...]

The parser never downloads. IDs: `guid:<slug>.<section>`; long sections are split into
`.p1`, `.p2`, ... (suffix is `.pN`, not `.N`, because `.N` collides with subsection numbers such as 3.2.1).
Each provision carries `meta.section`, `meta.paragraphs` (the Commission's own "(N)" paragraph numbers),
`meta.source_url` (landing page), `meta.file_url` (PDF), `meta.status`, `meta.date`, `meta.retrieved_at`.

### Not obtained / notes
- **High-risk guidelines final version**: does not exist yet (checked the Commission landing page and consultation page; both still show the May 2026 draft). The extracted text is draft: use `meta.status = "draft"` and cite accordingly. Paragraph numbering (63)-(450) may change.
- **Omnibus Q&A / factsheet**: the Commission has no omnibus-specific Q&A or factsheet beyond the news item and the Service Desk FAQ above (which only describes the *proposal*, not the adopted text). The Commission opinion on the Code of Practice (9 Jul 2026) and the Code signatory lists were not downloaded (not needed for classification).
- **Italian**: official Italian PDFs exist for the definition (.../118632), prohibited practices (.../118653) and GPAI (.../127086) guidelines and are downloaded as `*.it.pdf` (283 KB, 1.1 MB, 519 KB) but **not** converted to JSON (task scope: English only). The transparency guidelines, the high-risk draft and the Code of Practice are English only on the landing pages. All three 2025 guidelines exist in all 24 EU languages.
- Extraction quality: pypdf output. Footnote blocks are stripped heuristically; some footnote call-out digits remain glued to words ("service 5"), and a few justified-text word splits survive (e.g. "a s", "r of"). Superscripts are repaired for FLOP values (10^23, 10^25). `meta.paragraphs` lets you re-check against the PDF. Section headings with no body text of their own (pure parents) are not emitted.
- The transparency guidelines cite the definition guidelines as C(2025) 5053 and the prohibited-practices guidelines as C(2025) 5052; the Feb 2025 numbers (924, 884) are the original approval of the draft guidelines.

## Classification-relevant examples (for the test set)

Ids below are `guid:<slug>.<section>`; "(N)" is the Commission's paragraph number. Quotes are close paraphrase unless in quotation marks.

### AI system definition (`ai-system-definition-2025`)
- `.5.2` (42)-(51): systems outside the definition because they do not transcend "basic data processing": ML used to speed up/approximate classical optimisation (linear/logistic regression), physics-based simulations accelerated by ML, satellite bandwidth allocation optimisation; database queries ("find all customers who purchased a specific product in the last month"), spreadsheets without AI functions, a population-average calculator; descriptive sales dashboards / survey statistics (no recommendation of how to improve sales).
- `.5.2` (48)-(51): classical heuristics (a chess programme using minimax with heuristic evaluation, no learning from data) and "simple prediction systems" (predicting tomorrow's temperature with last week's average; always predicting the historical average price; static mean-resolution-time estimates) fall outside the definition.

### Prohibited practices (`prohibited-practices-2025`)
- `.7.2.2` (254): emotion recognition in the workplace. A call centre using webcams/voice recognition to track employees' emotions such as anger is prohibited; the same tool tracking *customers'* emotions is not caught by Art. 5(1)(f). Workplace includes recruitment candidates.
- `.7.2.2` (255): education. An app for learning a language online outside an institution is not prohibited; if an institution requires students to use it, it is. Gaze/eye-tracking in online exams that does not infer emotions is not prohibited; if it also detects emotional arousal or anxiousness it is.
- `.7.2.3` (256)-(263): the medical/safety exception is narrow (CE-marked medical devices; protection of life and health only). A system detecting burnout or depression at work/school, or measuring employees' anxiety, boredom or motivation, remains prohibited; general stress monitoring is not allowed. Pilot/driver fatigue detection is outside the "emotion recognition" definition (physical states, recital 18).
- `.7.4` (265)-(266): emotion recognition outside workplace/education (e.g. commercial, customer voice/keystroke analysis for personalised marketing) is not prohibited by 5(1)(f) but is high-risk, and may still hit 5(1)(a)/(b).
- `.3.5.2` (134): examples not likely to cause significant harm (out of 5(1)(a)/(b)): an anthropomorphic AI companion with affective computing that is not otherwise manipulative or reasonably likely to cause serious harm; a therapeutic chatbot using subliminal techniques to nudge users to quit smoking.
- `.8.3` (284)-(285): permissible biometric labelling/filtering: labelling biometric data to counter bias in recruitment; categorising patients by skin/eye colour for medical diagnosis (not biometric categorisation inferring race, beliefs, sexual orientation).
- `.5.3.2` (214): AI that supports a human assessment based on objective, verifiable facts linked to criminal behaviour (e.g. suspicious behaviour in a crowd, reviewed by a human) is outside Art. 5(1)(d).

### GPAI (`gpai-obligations-2025`)
- `.2.1` (17): indicative criterion for a GPAI model: training compute greater than 10^23 FLOP and able to generate language (text or audio), text-to-image or text-to-video; 10^23 FLOP is roughly a one-billion-parameter model trained on a large dataset (18).
- `.2.1` (20): examples: a text model trained on broad web data with 10^24 FLOP is likely a GPAI model; a 10^22 FLOP model that cannot do a wide range of tasks is not; models trained specifically for speech transcription, text-to-speech, image upscaling, inpainting (all 10^24 FLOP) or for playing chess/video games are not GPAI because they perform only a narrow set of tasks.
- `.2.3.1` / intro (5): GPAI model with systemic risk presumed above 10^25 FLOP cumulative training compute (Art. 51(2)); notification reports compute with two significant figures, e.g. 2.3x10^25 (31).
- `.3.1.2` (51): placing on the market includes: first availability via API, software library, public repository/hub download, cloud service, copy onto a customer's infrastructure, integration into a chatbot on a web interface or into a mobile app via app store.
- `.3.2` (60)-(62): downstream modifiers (e.g. fine-tuners) become GPAI providers only if the modification uses more than a third of the original model's training compute (fallback when unknown: a third of 10^23 FLOP, or of 10^25 FLOP if the original model has systemic risk).

### High-risk classification, draft (`high-risk-classification-2026-draft`)
- `general.II.2` (12): a system presented as broadly applicable "across a generality of contexts and functions" without consistently excluding high-risk uses is deemed to have a high-risk intended purpose; "merely asserting (for example in the terms of service) that high-risk uses are excluded is insufficient".
- `annex3.IV.2.7.1` (89)-(92): Art. 6(3) filter conditions are exhaustive, alternative, narrowly interpreted; profiling always stays high-risk. "Narrow procedural task": sorting incoming school/university applications by grade or educational level into predefined categories without evaluating suitability (recital 53: unstructured-to-structured data, document classification, duplicate detection).
- `annex3.IV.2.7.1` (93)-(94): categorisation that attributes scores/ranks or marks input as "useful"/"less useful" is not a narrow procedural task. Visa-file scanning, OCR indexing, filing into fixed folders, exact-duplicate detection and flagging incomplete forms can benefit from the filter if they do not rank, hide or suggest credibility assessments.
- `annex3.IV.2.7.1` (95)-(101): "improve the result of a previously completed human activity" (e.g. ex post improving a drafted text; must not substantially change the outcome); "detect decision-making patterns" (e.g. anomalies in a teacher's grading pattern, recital 53) requires a completed human assessment and proper human review.
- `annex3.IV.3.5.1` (280)-(281) and `annex3.IV.3.5.3`: Annex III 5(b) creditworthiness excludes systems used to detect financial fraud; `annex3.IV.3.4` (236): employment - targeted job ads, filtering/analysing applications and evaluating candidates are high-risk (Annex III 4(a)).

### Art. 50 transparency (`transparency-art50-2026`, final)
- `.3.1.1` (30)-(31): Art. 50(1) needs an AI system intended to interact directly with natural persons. Out of scope: rule-based auto-replies/out-of-office, spam filters, recommenders, automated translation/transcription, text/code auto-completion, backend decision support, AI-assisted product visualisation, closed industrial robots, machine-to-machine agent calls. In scope: AI agents that can interact with people (booking, negotiating, purchasing), AI avatars, social-media bots, coding agents interacting with people; they must disclose their artificial nature and on whose behalf they act.
- `.3.2.1` (42)-(45): the "obvious interaction" exception is interpreted restrictively (reasonably well-informed, observant, circumspect person; target audience incl. vulnerable persons). Exempt examples: AI code-assistant chatbots for professional developers; internal employee-facing HR/legal/IT assistants for trained AI-literate staff; interactive systems used only by trained health professionals for diagnosis support; home-appliance ambient AI limited to operating the appliance; AI NPCs in a single-player video game. Not exempt: a lifelike robotic companion pet; immersive VR/AR avatars with human-like voices; chatbots on online platforms/helpdesks whose replies users may perceive as human.
- `.3.2.2` (46)-(49): law-enforcement exception covers AI authorised by law to detect/prevent/investigate/prosecute crime (e.g. AI undercover agents), but not publicly available systems that allow reporting offences (police chatbots, AI fraud-reporting hotlines).
- `.4.1.3` (64)-(68): outside Art. 50(2) marking: rendered frames, playlists/recommenders that only select existing content, sensor data (smart meters, GPS), short sequences of numbers/symbols/letters (single words, captions, alt-text, UI labels), source code (SQL, YAML, JSON configs, APIs), machine-to-machine-only outputs, closed-loop production workflows.
- `.4.3` (89)-(93): Art. 50(2) exceptions: standard editing (grammar/spell-check, translation, formatting, minor crop/colour, red-eye, face blurring, noise reduction) vs. substantial changes needing marking (AI summaries, rewriting that changes meaning, object/person removal or insertion, face replacement, synthetic voice of a specific person, composite images).

### Code of Practice on AI-generated content (`code-of-practice-ai-content-2026`)
- Section 1 (providers, Art. 50(2)): Commitment 1 marking (`sec1.measure-1.1` machine-readable marking, `1.2` non-removal), Commitment 2 detection, 3 effectiveness/robustness/interoperability, 4 compliance. Section 2 (deployers, Art. 50(4)): `sec2.commitment-1` disclosure of deep fakes and published text with the common EU icon, `commitment-3` artistic/creative works, `commitment-4` human review/editorial control for text. Signing is voluntary; non-signatories must show compliance by other means.

### Omnibus
- `ai-omnibus-2026.2`: high-risk Annex III rules now apply from 2 December 2027, Annex I (products) from 2 August 2028; new prohibition on nudification / CSAM-generating systems (applies from 2 Dec 2026 per Commission AI Act pages); Art. 50 transparency obligations unchanged (apply from 2 Aug 2026; a grace period to 2 Dec 2026 for marking by systems already on the market is mentioned by secondary sources - verify against Regulation 2026/1744 text).
